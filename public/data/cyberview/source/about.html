What CyberView is

CyberView watches the world's public reporting on cyber security and turns it into one place a defender can use. Scripts collect from 6,977 government, vendor and news feeds in more than 30 languages, with sources for nearly every country (Canada's federal, provincial and municipal bodies, police, utilities and regional press among them, and the small states of Africa, the Caribbean and the Pacific), banks, central banks and financial regulators, critical-infrastructure operators and crypto security firms, official breach registers and SlowMist's archive of crypto hacks, standing news searches in local languages and several open databases every few minutes; CyberView AI (Claude models by Anthropic) researches what they find, writes it up with its sources, and a second AI agent checks it before it counts as reviewed. Nothing is drawn as a fact until it has been read from a real source.

It keeps growing. New sources, views and analyses are added continuously, and this page is generated from the same list the site is built from, so it always describes what CyberView does today: 1,255 events on file, 433,031 CVEs indexed, 56 power grid sources connected.

  • Seven-day globe of cyber events: Every checked cyber event of the last seven days on a 3D globe, by type (shape and colour-blind-safe colour) and severity, with what happened, why it matters, what to do and the sources. Arcs connect events only where a source states the link, or where a government or the investigating firm made an attribution, and say who said so.
  • Timeline and Correlation: The same events by day and attack surface, and as a correlation graph of shared actors, products and techniques, each with the question it answers and its caveats. Sector and attack surface come from fixed lists (the US CISA critical infrastructure sectors and a few more), each stated by the source or inferred by CyberView AI (a Claude model); events not yet classified are placed by keyword, and each view counts and labels them as keyword-assigned.
  • Continuous research loop: A loop runs every few minutes: it collects leads, groups them into stories ranked by how many independent publishers report them, sends researchers to new stories and reviewers to new events in parallel, and publishes. The target is under 15 minutes from a lead to a published, reviewed event. Routine bulletins and single unconfirmed headlines are closed by written rules, and unconfirmed reports appear only in a labelled 'being checked' list.
  • Official breach notices: Breach notices that organisations file with regulators are read straight from the registers that publish them (the California, Washington and Iowa attorneys general, the UK Information Commissioner's enforcement actions, and US SEC filings for material cybersecurity incidents once a contact address is set). Each new notice is a lead from a government source: a researcher reads it, cites the regulator's own page, and adds it to the map or to the event it confirms. Notices of the same breach on several registers are counted as one story.
  • Crypto hacks and exploits: Thefts from DeFi protocols, exchanges, bridges and wallets are read from SlowMist's public archive of crypto hacks, a blockchain security firm's database. Each new row is a lead that keeps what the database states: the project, the date, the amount lost, the attack method, the target type, the chain and the reference it lists. It is a fact to check, not a report: a researcher confirms it from the project's own statement or the reference source before anything reaches the map.
  • Events that keep up with the story: Published events are updated when new reporting adds a confirmation, a figure, an attribution or a patch, and go back to the second review after each change; developing stories and attackers' claims are followed up on a schedule. Corrections are logged publicly.
  • Vulnerabilities: Known-exploited flaws ranked for patching with US federal deadlines and fix steps from the vendor's own advisory; 433,031 CVEs since 1999 to look up and browse, each with the CyberView rating (the CVSS base score adjusted for exploitation evidence with CVSS's own Exploit Code Maturity metric, on the CVSS scale and bands) and an urgency priority that follows CISA's SSVC and BOD 26-04; the vendor's own advisory joined to each CVE (twelve vendors: severity and fixed versions as the vendor states them, and its own word when a flaw is exploited in the wild, which raises the rating); your own software list matched by version, in your browser, against NVD's affected-version ranges for every CVE that has them (not just the known-exploited ones) and never uploaded; and hunt guidance. Exploitation analyses by CyberView AI explain how a flaw is used and how to fix it, without exploit code.
  • Industrial (ICS/OT) advisories: An Industrial view on the Vulnerabilities page with every industrial control system and operational technology advisory of the last year from CISA (ICS and medical devices), Siemens, Schneider Electric, ABB, Phoenix Contact, CERT@VDE, Moxa and Mitsubishi Electric, read by a script from their own machine-readable feeds: affected products, CVEs, CVSS, what the advisory and CISA's known-exploited catalog say about exploitation, the critical infrastructure sectors, and a link to the advisory with its mitigations. Each CVE elsewhere on the page shows the industrial advisories that name it. Exploited and critical advisories, and high-scoring ones in energy, water, health and other lifeline sectors, are handed to CyberView AI to research.
  • Threat-report library mirror: A keyless mirror of ORKL's public library of historical threat-intelligence reports (orkl.eu) and its threat-actor catalogue, kept as metadata and links only by the mirror itself, which reads no report text and uses no AI model: for each report its title, authors, date, language, sources and named actors, with links to the report's page and the archived PDF and text on archive.orkl.eu, never the report body. The first run backfills the whole library, newest first; later runs add only what is new. It feeds no events and is drawn on no map. The Library page (in the top bar; its section strip also holds Actors in events and the Event archive) lets you search and filter every report and actor, fifty to a page, and open each record: a report links to its own ORKL record and PDF; an actor's record shows its aliases, tools, the reports in the library that name it, its ORKL profile and, when CyberView has it on file, its profile among the actors in events. A link to a report or an actor opens that record on the page of the list that holds it. Its totals, languages and most-named actors are also shown on the Status page. Collected every two hours by a script with no AI model; the AI-written summaries and stories on the same page come from the separate Library reports entry below.
  • Library reports (AI-written summaries and stories): Short summaries, fact cards and written stories about the mirrored ORKL threat reports and the threat actors they describe, shown on the Library page. A Haiku model reads each report's text in passing and returns a card of facts and short evidence quotes; the text is never stored, and what is kept is CyberView's own summary, facts and quotes of at most 160 characters, which stay in the data branch (public in the repository) and are never shown on the site. A separate Sonnet auditor re-checks every card against the report, its summary included, and a summary is shown only after that audit has passed it (the owner may audit a smaller share; unaudited cards then show no summary). A Sonnet model writes a story per threat actor from the fact cards only. A story is published only after script checks and a pass from a separate Sonnet review run that re-reads the original reports; every factual sentence cites its reports, and the original stays on ORKL. Everything on these pages is written by CyberView AI (a Claude model), the models have no tools, and the work starts with a small pilot, then works through the whole library. It is billed to CyberView's API account within its own monthly budget, so it never takes the usage the map needs. If a summary or story is wrong, use Report an error on it, or open an issue in the repository, and it is corrected or withdrawn; a story that rests on a card found wrong is taken down until a corrected version passes review.
  • Tools (under development): A Tools page for practical defender tools built on CyberView's data. It is under development: the page says so, offers nothing to download or run yet, and links to the tools that already work elsewhere on CyberView. Like About, Privacy and Terms it has its own search and an "On this page" contents list.
  • Power grid outages: A Grid view that sets everything else aside and shows only electrical grid outages from 56 grid operators and data sources (utilities, system operators, and the statewide or national counts that government emergency agencies publish, such as Pennsylvania's, Maryland's and Chile's, plus Japan's supply reserve per area from OCCTO): every outage of 100 or more customers, larger incidents tracked from start to end, load shedding and grid emergencies, which outages are unusual for the hour, which are confirmed by internet measurements, disaster alerts, demand data or news, and 30-day trends. A third tier lists power outages reported in the news in many languages, grouped by place and time with each publisher named and linked, found by a script and labelled as not checked by CyberView until CyberView AI's outage desk has checked them; they are never counted as incidents. CyberView AI checks the cause of big or corroborated incidents; an outage is never called an attack unless an official source says so. A rule-based audit checks every grid source each cycle (failing or frozen feeds, sudden falls in customers, misplaced points), and the status page lists its flags.
  • Context layers: Internet disruptions (IODA) and disaster alerts (GDACS) on the globe, drawn in neutral ink and always labelled as context, not cyber events; a strong internet disruption shows the outage desk's check when there is one.
  • Scam warnings by country: Official warnings about scams and phishing from government bodies, police forces, regulators and national cyber security centres in several countries and languages, read every two hours from the issuers' own feeds by a script with no AI model, and listed by country in the map page's Insights tab with the issuer named and a link only to the issuer's own page. The scam type (impersonation, investment, delivery, bank or SMS, tech support, romance, job, crypto) is assigned by a fixed keyword list and labelled so. They are warnings, not cyber events: never drawn on the globe or counted as events; a warning that describes a cyber incident is passed to the research loop as a lead.
  • Outage checks: An outage desk inside the research loop, where CyberView AI (a Claude model) checks outage reports that no one had checked: power outages reported in the news that no operator feed covers, country-level internet disruptions measured by IODA, and major outages on providers' own status pages. Each is read against the operator's, provider's or government's own page and credible reporting and marked confirmed (naming who confirmed it, with figures, times and cause only as a source states them), not confirmed (with the reason), or, when a source links it to a cyberattack, handed to the research loop as a lead, never published as an attack by itself. The Grid view and the internet disruption layer show the result next to the report, and reports not checked yet stay labelled as unchecked. A confirmed internet or service outage can become an outage event, with a second review like every other. The service outages come from the providers' own status pages (cloud, CDN, DNS, SaaS, payment and telecom), read by a script with no AI model; major or long incidents are listed under Service status in the map's layers panel, each labelled as the provider's own status page and not a cyber event, and the outage desk checks the biggest providers first.
  • Leak-site claims checked: A claims desk inside the research loop, where CyberView AI (a Claude model) checks the claims ransomware and extortion groups post on their leak sites, most notable victim first: hospitals and health, government, energy and other critical sectors, large companies, victims other reports already name, and groups whose claims have been confirmed before. For each claim it looks for the organisation's own statement, a regulator filing or a government notice, then credible reporting, and records one result: confirmed by the victim (an event with a second review), reported by two publishers but not confirmed (kept as an unverified claim, severity low and hidden by default), denied, nothing beyond the leak site (checked again later) or victim not identifiable. Events show the check as "Checked by CyberView AI (a Claude model)". Leak sites are never cited or linked, only the group's name and the claim's date are recorded, and stolen data is never opened or described.
  • Brief, patterns and trends: A daily brief and a pattern analysis written by CyberView AI from the week's events, and week-over-week trends by category, sector, country, actor and product.
  • Threat actor profiles: One profile per group named in CyberView's checked events, put together by a script, not written: the events a government or the investigating firm attributed to it (each saying who said so), the events whose reporting names it, and, kept apart and labelled as unconfirmed, what it only claimed on its leak site. Other names are joined only where MITRE ATT&CK lists them as aliases or an event's sources name them as one group, and each says which. Each profile lists sectors and countries targeted, CVEs and government advisories in its events, and links to them in the archive and to the group's record in the Library's actor catalogue. It sits under Library in the top bar, as Actors in events.
  • Archive: Every event CyberView has ever recorded (1,255 so far), searchable in pages of fifty, with RSS and JSON feeds; it sits under Library in the top bar, as Event archive. Every CVE an event names, in its CVE list or its text, links to its CVE.org and NVD records, to CISA's known-exploited catalog when CISA lists it, and to the Vulnerabilities page's own check, here and in the event's write-up on the map. Events that research finds after the fact (they happened more than a day before CyberView filed them) are routed here under Recently filed, with the day they happened and when they were filed, so the map's Just added list only ever shows new events.
  • Weekly tabletop exercise: Each week one verified event becomes a ready-to-run exercise with timed updates, discussion questions and what good looks like; the scenario is invented for practice and says so.
  • Community: Defenders' tips and questions, reviewed by fixed rules and then the CyberView AI moderator before they appear; never used as a source for an event.
  • Data CyberView would like to use: Every power grid and cyber security source CyberView would like to show but may not yet, because its terms restrict reuse, its robots.txt disallows the data, its site blocks automated readers, it needs a key or it could not be reached, each with the reason, the contact its operator publishes, a short request written for that operator and the order to ask in. CyberView never works around a block; a source is connected only once it is allowed.
  • Open about how it runs: A status page that answers first whether the site is healthy right now, followed by how the data flows (sources, leads, stories, closed by rule, read by AI agents, published, second-reviewed and on the map, each with its count for the last 24 hours and 7 days and the share it passes on) and where the data comes from (events of the week by source tier, collector, region and language, and the publishers they cite most) and what needs attention, with every detail one click away: every routine, when it last ran and what it found; an hourly pipeline check, a script with no AI model, that measures every stage from the collectors to the deploy against its limit, restarts a stage that has stopped, and checks the data itself (records vanishing from the status file, events recorded twice, events waiting over two hours for their second review, lead files or the data growing abnormally, feeds and collectors that keep failing, which are asked less often and proposed for the watch list instead of being dropped silently), listing every problem under what needs attention and opening one public GitHub issue per problem that lasts, closed again when it is healthy; an outside guardian that checks the live site and every stage every two hours and restarts what has stopped; link and security checks; an improver that tunes cost and quality within written limits, keeping each AI role on the least costly model that does it well and the most capable model only for the few jobs that need it; a measured-accuracy check that turns every past second review into a test set, so a change to a prompt or a model is judged by how it scores against real corrections, not by impression; and an efficiency report, worked out by a script with no AI model at every build: usage and idle runs per routine, leads and how many became events per feed, coverage by region, language and sector, and corrections by week, each saying how many days of data it rests on. Publishing never stops when the shared usage window is used up: the subscription comes first and a paid API fallback takes over only then, within a fixed monthly limit. The code and the job instructions are public.

How it is made

  • Collection: scripts read more than 280 government, vendor and news feeds in 28 languages, ask two independent news indexes (Bing News and GDELT) standing questions in many languages, keeping only links to the publishers' own pages, and read primary databases: CISA's known-exploited list, ENISA's EU Vulnerability Database, NIST NVD, FIRST EPSS, Have I Been Pwned, cloud providers' status pages and leak-site tracking. Security leads surfaced on X (Twitter) are taken only as starting points to check, never as evidence for an event. No AI is involved in this step, and no single search engine decides what CyberView sees.
  • Corroboration: leads about the same thing are grouped into one story, and stories reported by more independent publishers are researched first. Leak-site listings are attackers' claims: they are never published as facts.
  • Research: CyberView AI (Claude models by Anthropic, working as scheduled jobs) turns leads into events and writes every summary, brief, analysis and exercise on the site. They work under written rules that are published in full: facts only from pages actually read, original government and vendor documents preferred, specific source links required, outages never called attacks without an official source, attacker claims marked unverified.
  • Second review: every new or changed event is re-read against its sources by a different AI agent from the one that wrote it, which passes it, corrects it or flags it. Each event shows whether that review has happened.
  • Checking: a verification job re-checks recent events against their sources, the verify desk answers reader reports, an outside guardian checks the live site every two hours, and every build validates every record. Corrections are logged publicly.
  • Accuracy, cost and reliability: every correction the second review makes is kept as a test set, so changes to the AI prompts and models are judged by how they score against real past corrections, not by impression; each AI role runs on the least costly model that does its job well, with the most capable model kept for the few jobs that need it; and publishing never stops when the shared usage window is used up, because a paid fallback takes over automatically, after the subscription and within a fixed monthly limit. The status page shows what the second review is correcting now.
  • Severity follows exploitation: known-exploited flaws, public exploit code or an official "patch immediately" in widely used or internet-facing software are rated high.
  • Links and attribution on the globe: arcs connect events only where a source states the link (for example a breached supplier and a customer hit through it). Attribution arcs are drawn only when a government body or the investigating firm made the attribution, and name who made it; CyberView does not attribute attacks itself.
  • Vulnerabilities: the Vulnerabilities page ranks exploited flaws by an open score, compares the software versions you list against NIST NVD's affected ranges inside your browser, and shows check and fix steps that an AI job takes from the vendor's own advisory. Version data can lag or be incomplete: confirm against the vendor advisory before you rely on a "not affected".
  • Community feed: visitors' posts are reviewed before they appear, first by fixed rules and then by the CyberView AI moderator (a Claude model). They are the authors' own words, not CyberView's reporting, and are never used as a source for an event; a credible tip is passed to the research jobs to verify from real sources.
  • Library reports: the Library page lists public threat-intelligence reports mirrored from ORKL (metadata and links only; the mirror reads no report text). Separately, a Library job, which runs when its scheduled job is switched on, has CyberView AI (a Claude model) read each report's text in passing; it never stores the text and keeps only its own summary, facts and short evidence quotes (at most 160 characters, never shown on the site; they are kept on the data branch, which is public in the repository, so they can be read there). A summary is shown only after a separate AI audit has checked it against the report. Stories on the Library page are written by CyberView AI (a Claude model) from those fact cards only, and are published only after script checks and a pass from a separate AI review run that re-reads the original reports. Every factual sentence cites its reports and every story links to the originals, which stay on ORKL and belong to their authors. If a story or a summary is wrong, use Report an error on it, or open an issue; a wrong entry is corrected or withdrawn.
  • Tabletop exercises: each week an AI job turns one verified event into a practice exercise. The real facts carry their sources; the scenario for your organisation is invented for practice and says so.
  • Every routine and its last run are shown on the status page; the full history is in the archive and the public repository.

AI-assisted research can make mistakes. Read information, not advice before relying on an entry.

Corrections and removal requests

If an entry, or a Library summary or story, is wrong, use Report an error on it, or open an issue in the repository. The verification job checks every report against the sources; when an original contradicts an entry, the entry is fixed and the correction is logged publicly with what changed.

If you are an organisation or person named in an entry and believe it is inaccurate, unfair, infringes copyright or breaches your privacy, contact us privately (below) with the event link and what is wrong. We review requests promptly, correct or remove content that is inaccurate or unlawful, and tell you the outcome. Attacker claims the named organisation has not confirmed are labelled as such and hidden by default; an organisation's denial is recorded when it is published.

Private contact: cyberviewthreats@gmail.com

Security disclosure

If you find a security problem in CyberView, please report it privately through GitHub's private vulnerability reporting, not in a public issue. Good-faith research that respects these rules is welcome: do not access other people's accounts or data, degrade the service, or use social engineering; give us reasonable time to fix the issue before disclosure. We do not pay bounties. Machine-readable details: security.txt.

Accessibility

We aim to meet WCAG 2.2 level AA. Event types are shown by both colour and shape using a colour-blind-safe palette, the map and lists work with a keyboard (press ? on the map for shortcuts), motion respects your reduced-motion setting, and every map event is also listed as text in the feed. Known limitation: the map and charts are visual summaries; the feed, archive and exports carry the same information as text. If something is hard to use, please tell us.
